# External-API example

Calling outside services from the TEE: over TLS, past tee-node's 2 s, with partial results; and putting the TEE's answer on chain, signed.
`GET_PRICE` asks three public APIs for a pair's price: the extension answers `status 2` at once, posts each source's price as a partial result as it arrives, then their median, signed with the TEE key, as the final result.
Anyone can submit that to the contract, which checks the signature and keeps the latest price per pair.

> [!WARNING]
> An example, not a production oracle.
> A price lands seconds after the request when all goes well, and may already be 10 minutes old when the contract stores it.
> One TEE answers each request, and its operator sees the price first and may withhold it.
> [Limits](#limits) lists them all.

## Layout

As the [template](../../template/README.md):

- `cmd/extension/main.go`: the entrypoint; it passes the sign port, which long-running results are posted through.
- `internal/app`: the ops, the sources and the median, and their tests.
- `conformance/`: golden fixtures, generated by `internal/app/fixtures_test.go`.
- `contracts/`: the entrypoint contract, `InstructionSender`, with `getPrice`, `submitPrice` and `latestPrice`, its scripts and its forge tests.
- `Dockerfile`: the TEE image; it differs from the template's only in the lines after a `# per extension:` comment.

## Ops

| Op type | Op command | Channel | Payload | Partial results | Final result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `PRICES` | `GET_PRICE` | instruction | `abi.encode(string pair)` | `abi.encode(string source, uint256 price)` | `abi.encode(string pair, uint256 price, uint8 decimals, uint8 sources, bytes signature)` |
| `PRICES` | `GET_PRICE_DIRECT` | direct | JSON `{"pair"}` | JSON `{"source", "price"}` | JSON `{"pair", "price", "decimals", "sources"}` |

The pairs are `BTC/USD`, `ETH/USD` and `FLR/USD`.
Prices are integers with 8 decimals; JSON carries them as decimal strings.
`sources` is how many answered.

An unknown pair answers `error: unknown pair` at once, and a payload that doesn't decode `error: invalid payload`.
When fewer than two sources answer, the final result is `status 0` with `error: too few sources answered`.

A caller sends `GET_PRICE` through `InstructionSender.getPrice(pair)`, paying at least `quoteFee(OP_TYPE_PRICES, OP_COMMAND_GET_PRICE)`; the contract keeps any excess.
The results land on the TEE's proxy; [on chain](#on-chain) says how the final one gets to the contract.
The TEE answers each instruction once: delivered again, it answers `error: instruction already answered`.
`GET_PRICE_DIRECT` is posted to the proxy, as the [template](../../template/README.md#my_direct_command) shows.
Its answer is unsigned: tee-proxy picks a direct action's id, so a signed answer could pass as an instruction's.

## Trusting the sources

The sources are Coinbase, Kraken and Bitstamp, fixed in [`internal/app/sources.go`](internal/app/sources.go).
They are part of the attested image: anyone rebuilding it sees which APIs the TEE trusts, and the operator can't point it elsewhere.

The operator runs the machine and its network, so every answer is treated as hostile until checked:

- Only `https` URLs, verified against the CA bundle in the image, which is attested too.
- No redirects followed: a source can't send the TEE to another host, or off TLS.
- Each answer is capped at 64 KiB and must hold a positive decimal price.
- A source that fails is left out, and its error stays inside: an answer may carry anything, and logs are public.
- The median needs two of the three, so one source down or wrong doesn't move it.
- The caller names a pair from a fixed list, mapped to each source's own symbol, so caller input never reaches a URL.

## On chain

`getPrice` records each request: its instruction id, the pair, the TEE it went to, and its block's timestamp, `requestedAt`.
The TEE signs its final answer with the TEE key, through tee-node's sign port, over

```
abi.encode(bytes32 "PRICES_PRICE_V1", bytes32 instructionId, keccak256(abi.encode(pair, price, decimals, sources)))
```

and appends the signature, with `V` 27 or 28, to the result.
Anyone may then call `submitPrice(instructionId, result)` with the final result's `data`, read from the TEE's proxy at `GET /action/result/<instructionId>`.
The contract stores the price only if:

- it requested that instruction, and no answer to it was submitted yet;
- the submission comes at most `MAX_PRICE_DELAY`, 10 minutes, after the request, by block time;
- the pair is the one requested;
- the signature recovers to the TEE the request went to, a production TEE of this extension, while the extension isn't emergency-paused.

`latestPrice(pair)` returns the stored price with its `requestedAt`.
An answer to an older request than the stored price's is accepted, and not stored.

The instruction id makes each signature good for one request, on one chain, at one contract: it arrives with the instruction, signed by the data providers, and the contract accepts it once.
So the extension needs no chain id.
`PRICES_PRICE_V1` keeps the signature from meaning anything else, such as one of tee-node's own.

The TEE answers each instruction once per boot.
tee-node doesn't remember instructions, and the operator could otherwise deliver one again, fetch anew, and submit the price it likes.

### Limits

- **Latency.** The instruction waits for the data providers to sign it, the sources take up to 5 s, and someone must read the result and submit it: seconds when all goes well.
  Not for anything that needs a price this second.
- **Freshness.** The bound is in chain time only.
  The TEE fetched the price between the request's block and the submission's, at most 10 minutes apart; it doesn't use its own clock, which nothing guarantees.
  So a stored price may be 10 minutes old when stored, and ages from there: check `requestedAt`.
- **One TEE.** Each request goes to one random TEE.
  Its operator runs the proxy, sees the price first, and may withhold it; the caller then asks again, and may get the same TEE.
  A stronger design asks several TEEs and takes a quorum of their signed answers.
- **Liveness.** Nothing is stored until someone submits; a request nobody answers in time is lost.
- **Availability.** The contract accepts any production TEE of the extension, even one whose availability check has lapsed; check `getAvailabilityCheckValidity` too if that matters.
- **Block time.** A validator may set a block's timestamp up to 10 s ahead of its clock.
- **Sources.** Their own timestamps aren't checked: a source serving an old price counts as one of three.

## Time

tee-node waits 2 s for an answer, and three APIs can take longer, so the extension answers `status 2` at once and fetches in the background, each source for up to 5 s.
The partial results get statuses 4, 5 and 6, in the order the sources answer; the final result is `1`, or `0`.
tee-proxy keeps the highest status, so a consumer waits for the final result, and times out: a restart in between leaves the action pending forever.

`GET_PRICE_DIRECT` needs no replay protection: a replay fetches again and changes nothing.
Each one still costs a request to every source, and all requests share their rate limits.

## Testing

```bash
go test ./examples/external-api/...                    # the sources stubbed over TLS, and the fixtures are current
go test ./examples/external-api/internal/app -update   # rewrite the fixtures after changing them
make test                                              # also runs the contract's forge tests
fce conformance ./examples/external-api                # build the binary and check it from the outside
fce image-check ./examples/external-api                # build the image, then check it from the outside
```

The binary fetches from the real sources, so a known pair's result isn't fixed, and the fixtures cover only what needs no network: an unknown pair and a bad payload, on both channels.
The unit tests stub the sources over TLS with answers they really gave, and cover the partials, the median, and each way a source fails: an error status, a redirect, an oversized or malformed answer.
They sign through a fake sign port, and check the signature recovers to its key.
The forge tests check `submitPrice` against each refusal, and both sides pin the same message hash, so neither can change the format alone.
